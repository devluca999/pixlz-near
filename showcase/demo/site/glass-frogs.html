Glass frogs: the see-through singers of the cloud forest

Field notes · Pixlz demo page (written for the video; not a real publication)

Glass frogs live along fast streams in the cloud forests of Central and South America. Most of them are no longer than a paperclip, lime green on top, and they spend the night calling from leaves that hang over the water.

Why you can see their hearts

The skin on a glass frog's belly is so clear that you can watch its heart beat and its stomach fill. From above, the frog looks like a leaf. From below, light passes straight through it, so its outline blurs against the bright leaf and a hungry bird looks right past it.

When they sleep, glass frogs pull most of their red blood cells into the liver and pack them tight, which makes the frog up to twice as transparent. When they wake, the blood flows back out.

Fathers on guard

Females lay their eggs on the underside of a leaf above the stream. In many species the father stays with the clutch for weeks, keeping the eggs wet and chasing off wasps. When the tadpoles hatch, they drop into the water below.

Where to find them

Look for them at night, after rain, with a red light that doesn't startle them. Listen for a short, high whistle from the leaves above a stream; the singer is almost always right there, if you can see it.
